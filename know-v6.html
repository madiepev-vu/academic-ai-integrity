Why AI hallucinates: How language models really work

Language models don't "know" or "understand" in the way humans do. Instead, they learn patterns from huge amounts of text and then predict the next word, one word at a time. Think of it like autocomplete on your phone—except trained on billions of sentences.

👁️ How hallucinations happen

1

Prediction, not retrieval. The model does not search a library of facts. It generates text based on statistical patterns. That means it can invent details that are fluent and convincing.

2

Connecting dots without a source. When a question isn't exactly in its training data, the model fills the gap by combining fragments and language patterns. That can create a response that looks like a fact, even if no real evidence supports it.

3

Plausible, polished, and hard to verify. Hallucinations often sound complete and detailed, which makes them easy to trust. The real test is whether the claim is backed by a verifiable source, not whether it sounds familiar.

Key takeaway: AI outputs are drafts, not facts. Always verify claims against reliable sources before using them in your work—whether in essays, research, or assignments.

Evaluating tools: ethics, vision, and values alignment

Not all AI tools are the same. Before you adopt one for coursework, it's worth asking: Who made this? What is their goal? Does this match my values?

What to research about an AI tool

🏢 Who's behind it?

Click to see what to look for →

Is it an academic research team, a startup, a big tech company, or an open-source community? Each has different incentives (profit, impact, openness, or a mix).

🎯 What's their stated mission?

Click to see what to look for →

Do they prioritize safety, accessibility, accuracy, or commercial growth? Read their blog or "About" page. Are they transparent?

🔒 Data and privacy

Click to see what to look for →

Do they store your inputs? Can you delete your data? Is there a student/educational version with stronger privacy? Read the privacy policy (seriously).

♻️ Environmental & social impact

Click to see what to look for →

Have they published data on carbon footprint or model training? Do they address bias in their outputs? Transparency here is rare but increasingly expected.

Questions to ask before adoption

  • Does this tool align with my values? If you care about privacy, avoid tools that share your data. If you care about environmental impact, favor tools that prioritise efficiency or publish carbon reports.
  • Is this tool appropriate for the task? A document-chat tool (ChatPDF, Explain Paper) is better for verifiable text. A general chatbot (ChatGPT) is better for brainstorming or drafting prompts.
  • What's my fallback plan? If the tool breaks, changes its terms, or becomes unavailable, can you still complete your work using other resources?
  • Have I checked for bias? Test it with diverse prompts and sources. Does it represent different perspectives fairly?

Values to compare

  • Environmental impact: look for energy efficiency reporting, small model options, or published carbon estimates.
  • Data privacy: look for GDPR compliance, clear data retention policies, and the ability to delete user inputs.
  • Transparency: look for model cards, published audits, or open-source code that explain how the system works.
  • AI vision: look for evidence that the company values responsible use, education, and harm reduction rather than purely growth or engagement.
Try this: Pick a tool you're considering (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Jenni.ai, etc.). Go to their company blog or research page and find their stated values or safety priorities. You don't have to agree, but you should know where they stand.

Example research: ChatGPT vs Google Gemini

C

ChatGPT (OpenAI)

Who is behind it? OpenAI is a research lab and capped-profit company founded in 2015, with a mission to ensure artificial general intelligence benefits all of humanity.

Vision: OpenAI publishes safety research and model cards, and frames its work around long-term alignment and equitable access, though it remains a proprietary model provider.

Privacy: OpenAI may retain user prompts and outputs to improve models unless you opt out via organisational settings. Free users should assume interactions may be logged.

Environmental impact: OpenAI has shared some efficiency goals and model scaling research, but it does not publish a detailed carbon footprint report for every model.

Transparency note: OpenAI publishes research papers and summaries, but the core GPT model weights are not open source.
G

Google Gemini

Who is behind it? Gemini is developed by Google / DeepMind and is part of Google's broader AI product strategy, built on Google's AI principles and product ecosystem.

Vision: Google positions Gemini as a general assistant that is helpful, safe, and grounded in Google’s existing services, with a strong emphasis on product integration and trusted usage.

Privacy: Gemini is governed by Google's account and privacy policies, including EU data protection for European users. Google offers control settings for data collection and use.

Environmental impact: Google publishes sustainability goals and claims carbon-neutral operations for its data centres, but specific Gemini training emissions are not fully detailed.

Transparency note: Google shares many research papers and principles, but the Gemini model itself is not open source.

Overview of relevant AI tools

Below is a curated overview of conversational AI tools relevant to academic work. Most offer a free version with core functionality, alongside a paid tier for advanced features. Select a tool to see a simulated example of what it looks like in practice.

GPT

ChatGPT

Use for: brainstorming prompts, drafting outlines, generating questions, and explaining concepts in plain language.

Benefits: very flexible and easy to use. Great for starting a task or exploring different angles.

Tip: use it for ideas, but verify any claim before citing it.
Gem

Google Gemini

Use for: structured planning, reasoning tasks, and synthesising information with a polished assistant experience.

Benefits: strong enterprise support and integration with Google ecosystems. Good for preparing teaching materials and summaries.

Tip: confirm their EU privacy policy and whether they support educational use cases.
P

Perplexity

Use for: scholarly research, source discovery, and citation-backed summaries.

Benefits: quick literature scanning with source clues, good for early-stage research.

Tip: always follow citations to the original article.
PDF

ChatPDF

Use for: asking questions about a specific paper, checking definitions, and clarifying sections.

Benefits: reduces hallucination risk when you stick to the uploaded document.

Tip: use it when you want precise support for a single source.
Co

Microsoft Copilot

Use for: drafting documents, summarising emails, and generating ideas inside Word, Teams, and Outlook.

Benefits: integrates with familiar Microsoft apps and works well when you need productivity support.

Tip: check whether your institution has a Copilot education agreement before using it with coursework.
NB

Google NotebookLM

Use for: turning notes and uploaded documents into summaries, explanations, and study workflows.

Benefits: good for knowledge management when you want a guided research assistant.

Tip: review Google’s document privacy terms for NotebookLM before uploading school work.
EP

Explain Paper

Use for: interpreting research papers, extracting key points, and understanding methods quickly.

Benefits: useful for science reading and literature review support with more document grounding.

Tip: compare the summary with the original paper to catch missing nuance.
J

Jenni AI

Use for: generating paragraph drafts, improving structure, and rewriting text for clarity.

Benefits: supports academic writing with editable output and quick revision help.

Tip: always verify facts and citations after using a writing assistant.
